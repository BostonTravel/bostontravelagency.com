Chowder, cannoli and the neighborhoods that made them

Boston Food, Neighborhoods & Food Tours

Boston's food tells the story of who came here. The Puritans brought baked beans and brown bread. The sea brought cod, clams, oysters and lobster. Then came waves of immigrants: the Irish, the Italians who made the North End their own, Portuguese fishing families, and Chinese families who built one of the oldest Chinatowns in the country.

The result is a city where you can eat a 200-year-old oyster bar's chowder for lunch, a cannoli on Hanover Street for dessert and dim sum the next morning. Here is what to eat, where to find it, and the tours that bring it all together.

The North End: Boston's Little Italy

The North End is Boston's oldest neighborhood. Paul Revere lived here, but by the early 1900s it was home to tens of thousands of Italian immigrants, and their families' bakeries, cafes, salumerias and red-sauce restaurants still line Hanover and Salem Streets.

Each summer the neighborhood throws a series of religious feasts, the biggest being the Saint Anthony's Feast in late August, held since 1919, with processions, music and food stands filling the streets. Any time of year, the best way to taste the North End is on foot, one small plate at a time.

The great cannoli debate

Ask ten Bostonians where to get a cannoli and you will start an argument. Mike's Pastry, with its famous white boxes tied with string, draws the longest lines. Modern Pastry, across the street, has a fiercely loyal following that swears by its shells filled to order. Many locals will quietly point you to Bova's, open around the clock. A food tour lets you try more than one and pick your own side.

Beyond the North End

Back Bay. Built on filled land in the 1800s, the Back Bay is all Victorian brownstones and tree-lined streets. Newbury Street is the place for sidewalk patios, cafes and people watching, and Boylston Street has some of the city's best-known restaurants.

South End. The largest Victorian row house neighborhood in the country is also one of Boston's best dining areas. Tremont Street is restaurant row, and the SoWa Open Market draws crowds on summer Sundays.

Chinatown. Compact and packed with flavor, from dim sum and bakeries to hand-pulled noodles and late-night hot pot.

Seaport. Boston's newest neighborhood, rising on old wharves, with seafood restaurants and rooftop bars overlooking the harbor.

Beacon Hill. Gas lamps, cobblestones and Charles Street's cozy cafes and neighborhood restaurants.

Cambridge. Across the river, Harvard Square and Central Square mix student favorites with some of the most creative cooking in the region.

Local tips

  • Come hungry. Most food tours add up to a full meal.
  • Tell your guide about allergies or dietary needs when you book.
  • Many North End restaurants are tiny and some are cash only. Reservations help on weekends.
  • Try chowder at a classic oyster bar and a lobster roll on the waterfront, then compare notes.
